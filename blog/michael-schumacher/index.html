    Before Lewis Hamilton, there was one name for the greatest driver in Formula 1. Michael Schumacher won seven world titles and 91 races, and he changed what people expected from a driver: fitness, endless testing and working with the team as hard as on the track.

    Arrival

    His debut came at Spa in 1991, standing in for a Jordan driver at short notice. He qualified seventh, which nobody expected, and Benetton signed him before the next race. His first win came at Spa a year later.

    Benetton titles

    He won the championship with Benetton in 1994 and again in 1995. The 1994 title was settled in Adelaide, when he and Damon Hill collided and both retired, leaving Michael champion by one point. That pattern, brilliant and sometimes ruthless, would follow him through his career.

    Rebuilding Ferrari

    In 1996 he moved to Ferrari, a team that hadn’t won the drivers’ title since 1979. Together with Jean Todt, Ross Brawn and designer Rory Byrne, he rebuilt it piece by piece.

    It wasn’t quick. In 1997 he collided with Jacques Villeneuve at the final race in Jerez while fighting for the title, and was excluded from that year’s championship results. In 1999 he broke his leg in a crash at Silverstone.

    Five in a row

    Then it clicked. In 2000 he won Ferrari’s first drivers’ title in 21 years, and he won again in 2001, 2002, 2003 and 2004. In 2004 he won 13 of the 18 races.

    The hard edges

    Part of his story is the moments that went too far: Adelaide 1994, Jerez 1997, and parking his car at Rascasse in Monaco qualifying in 2006, which got him sent to the back of the grid. I don’t think you can tell his story honestly without them. They came from the same total will to win that made him great.

    Retirement and return

    He retired at the end of 2006, after a close title fight with Fernando Alonso. In 2010 he came back with Mercedes for three seasons. The results were modest, but there were flashes: a podium in Valencia in 2012, and the fastest lap in qualifying in Monaco the same year, although a grid penalty meant he didn’t start from pole.

    Some of that Mercedes groundwork helped lay the foundations for the team that dominated the years after he left.

    Since

    In December 2013, he suffered a serious head injury in a skiing accident. His family has kept his health private ever since, and I think that’s theirs to keep. His son Mick raced in F1 for Haas in 2021 and 2022.

    When he retired, he held the records for titles, wins and poles. Some of those have since been matched or passed, but what he built at Ferrari still stands out.
